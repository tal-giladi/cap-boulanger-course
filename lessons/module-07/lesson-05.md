# 07.5 · Baking, Cooling, Staling and Storage

This lesson follows the bread from the moment the oven door closes to the moment it is eaten or thrown away: what happens in the oven, how to choose temperature and time, how to unload and cool it, why it goes stale and how to store it. You finish with a three-way storage test on your own bread.

## Why it matters

Baking turns a fragile foam into bread in 20 to 50 minutes, and a few degrees or minutes too many or too few show at once in the crust and the crumb. After the oven the bread keeps changing: it cools, its crust crackles, then it stales within a day or two. The référentiel asks you to choose a baking temperature for each product, explain how the dough changes in the oven and the bread after it, compare oven types and identify the main bread defects (S3.1.6), and to condition and store products so they keep their quality (C2.4). A real EP1 paper (2019) asked candidates to sort the advantages and drawbacks of three oven types (see [The CAP Boulanger Exam](../../references/cap-exam.md)).

## How it works

### What happens in the oven

![Illustrative crust and core temperature curves for a baguette at 250 °C: the surface reaches 100 °C within a few minutes, stays there while steam keeps it wet, then rises past 150 °C and colours; the core passes 46-50 °C (yeast dies, oven spring ends), 55-60 °C (starch gelatinises), 70-80 °C (gluten sets, ethanol leaves) and levels off just under 100 °C](../../assets/m07-baking-curve.svg)

| Phase | Core temperature | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| Oven spring | up to about 46-50 °C | gas expands, CO₂ leaves solution, yeast works fast then dies: the loaf grows ([04.1](../module-04/lesson-01.md), lesson [07.4](lesson-04.md)) |
| Crumb forms | about 55-80 °C | starch swells and gelatinises; gluten proteins set; ethanol evaporates (about 78 °C); the foam becomes a sponge ([02.3](../module-02/lesson-03.md)) |
| Crumb finishes | just under 100 °C | water keeps evaporating, so the core cannot pass 100 °C; the crumb dries to its final texture |
| Crust | surface above 100 °C | once dry, the surface heats far above 100 °C and colours: Maillard reactions between sugars and proteins, then caramelisation, roughly from 150 °C; aroma and flavour |

The core of a lean loaf should reach at least about 90 °C (lesson [05.4](../module-05/lesson-04.md)); a baguette gets there early, so its bake time is decided by the crust colour.

### Choosing temperature and time

The rule: **the bigger, denser or richer the product, the lower the temperature and the longer the bake**. A small piece must colour before it dries out; a large one must cook through before the crust burns. Sugar, milk, butter and eggs brown fast, so rich doughs bake cooler.

| Product (typical starting points; the sheet wins) | Oven | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Baguette 350 g, rolls 60 g (PC-02) | 250 °C deck, steam | 22 min; rolls 15 min |
| Bâtard or boule 400-500 g | 230-240 °C, steam | 25-35 min |
| Campagne 500 g baked (CA-01) on levain | 230 °C, steam | 35-45 min |
| Large loaf 1 kg and more, rye breads | 220-230 °C, steam | 45-60 min |
| Pain de mie in a tin | about 200 °C, no steam | 30-40 min |
| Viennoiserie on trays | convection oven, about 170-190 °C | 12-20 min |

**Programming**: deck ovens change temperature slowly. Plan products from the hottest to the coolest through the morning, or give each product its own deck. Home ovens recover heat slowly after the door opens: reheat 10 minutes between loads.

### Défournement: four checks

1. **Colour**: even, golden to deep brown for lean bread; underside coloured too.
2. **Sound**: a knock on the base sounds hollow.
3. **Weight**: lighter than the pâton by the expected baking loss (about 20 % for a baguette as a planning figure, measured in lesson [06.3](../module-06/lesson-03.md); at least about 15 % for most breads).
4. **Core**: on a test loaf, at least about 90 °C; lean loaves usually finish at 96-98 °C.

### Ressuage: the bread is not finished yet

Out of the oven, the crust is dry and hot and the crumb is full of steam. During ressuage steam leaves through the crust, the crumb sets as it cools and firms, and the bread loses a little more weight. Cut too early, the crumb is gummy. As the crust cools quickly it contracts and cracks: the bread "sings".

Rules of ressuage (C2.4):

- on **racks** (grilles), in a single layer, with air all round, never on a solid shelf, never stacked;
- away from steam, the proofing cabinet and raw dough;
- **30 minutes minimum** for baguettes and rolls; 1-2 hours for large loaves; dense rye loaves longer;
- bagged, sliced or packed only when cold.

### Staling: two different processes

**The crumb firms** because gelatinised starch slowly **retrogrades**: the starch chains (mainly amylopectin) line up again into crystals and push out water. The crumb becomes firm, crumbly and dull-tasting. Retrogradation is fastest at refrigerator temperature: in a study of 25, 4 and −18 °C, bread stored at 4 °C staled fastest, while at −18 °C crystals only grew from those already there (Aguirre et al., 2011). **Never keep bread in the fridge** unless mould is the bigger risk.

**The crust softens** because water moves from the moist crumb to the dry crust: within hours a crisp baguette crust turns leathery, faster in humid air or in a plastic bag.

Staling is partly reversible: reheating above about 60 °C re-gelatinises the starch, which is why a day-old loaf refreshed in the oven for a few minutes tastes fresh again, for a short time.

| Slows staling | Speeds it up |
|---|---|
| Large loaves (less crust per gram of crumb) | Small, thin products (baguettes stale within a day) |
| Levain and long fermentation (acids; also slows mould, [04.1](../module-04/lesson-01.md)) | Short, direct fermentation |
| Higher hydration; fat and sugar (pain de mie, viennoiserie) | Lean, dry dough; over-baking |
| Freezing at −18 °C | Refrigeration around 4 °C |

### Conservation and conditionnement

| Method | Crust | Crumb | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open on a rack or in a paper bag | stays crisp longest | dries and firms | crusty bread eaten the same day |
| Sealed plastic bag | goes soft within hours | stays soft longer; mould sooner in warm rooms | pain de mie; sliced bread |
| Fridge (about 4 °C) | soft | stales fastest | avoid for bread |
| Freezer (−18 °C), cooled, wrapped airtight | refresh in a hot oven | close to fresh after thawing; up to about three months | anything not eaten within a day or two |

In the bakery, finished products go to the shop on clean racks or in baskets, protected from customers' hands. Semi-finished products (pâte fermentée, frozen dough, par-baked bread) are **dated, wrapped in film and stored in their place** (cold room, freezer), and products packed for sale carry the label information covered in lesson [02.11](../module-02/lesson-11.md). Whether a business that calls itself a boulangerie may freeze dough or bread at all is a legal question, answered in lesson [09.1](../module-09/lesson-01.md); what to do with unsold bread is in lesson [18.2](../module-18/lesson-02.md).

### The ovens

| Oven | How it heats | Advantages | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Four à sole (deck) | bread directly on a hot stone or steel floor; heat stored in the mass | strong bottom heat and oven spring, artisanal crust; steam | slow to heat and to change temperature; needs a loader; floor space |
| Four ventilé (convection) | a fan blows hot air over trays | quick to heat, easy to use, small footprint, suited to viennoiserie | surface limited to 60 × 40 cm trays; dries bread crust; less suited to bread |
| Four à chariot (rack) | a whole rack of trays turns in hot air | time saved at loading (one rack in), large output | bread baked on trays or nets looks less artisanal; investment |
| Four à bois (wood-fired) | heat from a wood fire stored in the masonry | traditional skill, commercial argument, rustic look | fuel to manage, experience needed, slow and uneven to control |

## Worked example

Saturday at Boulangerie Au Pain de la Halle: the two-deck oven must bake 24 baguettes PC-02, 10 campagne boules CA-01 (pâton 590 g, ordered at 500 g baked, measured loss 15 %, from the [module 6](../module-06/lesson-01.md) [workbook](../../projects/m06-calculation-workbook.md)) and 4 pains de mie in tins. The campagne goes to a restaurant tomorrow at 11:00.

**1. Programme the oven.** The deck oven changes temperature slowly. Top deck at 250 °C for the baguettes, then rolls; bottom deck at 230 °C for the campagne (40 minutes, steam), then left to fall to about 200 °C for the pains de mie (no steam, 35 minutes). Each product bakes on the deck nearest its temperature without waiting for an oven to cool.

**2. Défournement of the campagne.** Colour deep brown, base hollow. Weigh two boules straight out: 512 g and 515 g. At 1 hour: 503 g and 505 g. Loss from 590 g: (590 − 504) ÷ 590 ≈ **14.6 %**, close to the measured 15 %: the boules meet the 500 g ordered. Core of a test boule: 97 °C.

**3. Ressuage and conditioning.** The boules cool 2 hours on racks, then go into paper bags (crust stays crisp), dated, in the dry store away from the proofing cabinet. Not in plastic (the crust would soften overnight) and not in the cold room (the crumb would stale fastest).

**4. The pains de mie.** Out of the tins at once (left in, steam condenses and the sides go soft and dented), cooled 2 hours on racks, sliced and bagged in plastic only when cold, dated.

**5. A defect on the baguettes.** The second load of baguettes has burnt, hard bases: **pain ferré**. The top deck's sole was still at 265 °C after the steam cycle had been run empty. Correction now: the next load goes on a baking net or a second tray layer; prevention: check the sole with the thermometer before loading and lower the bottom heat on that deck.

**6. Advice for the sales staff (C4.2).** "Campagne: keeps two to three days in its paper bag thanks to the levain; refresh a few minutes in a hot oven. Baguette: best within the day; freeze what you will not eat."

## Practice

You bake six identical bâtards, cool them properly, store them three ways and compare them after one and two days.

> [!WARNING]
> The oven is at 240 °C with steam. Steam only into a preheated metal tray (never a glass dish), long dry glove, pour and stand back. Probe the core with the tray on a heat-proof surface, not inside the oven, and cut cooled bread on a board with the blade moving away from your hand.

### You need

- Minimum: one 1 kg PD-01 dough and the baking setup that worked best for you in lesson [07.4](lesson-04.md), scale, probe thermometer, oven thermometer, wire rack, two paper bags, four sealable plastic bags, a marker, a bread knife, a board, the [bake log](../../templates/bake-log.md) and the [quality rubric](../../templates/quality-rubric.md).
- Professional equivalent: cooling racks on trolleys, paper bags and film, labelled storage in the dry store, cold room and freezer.

### Ingredients

PD-01 on 1 kg of flour (flour 1,000 g, water 650 g, salt 18 g, fresh yeast 15 g or instant 5 g; total 1,683 g) → 6 bâtards of 270 g; the remaining 63 g as a test roll.

### In Israel

Checked 2026-10-08.

- **Summer humidity and heat.** On the coast in summer, a crisp crust goes leathery within hours and bread moulds sooner at room temperature: eat lean bread the same day and freeze the rest, sliced, once fully cooled. In an air-conditioned room the crumb dries faster: a paper bag inside a bread box is a good compromise.
- **Paper bags.** Paper bread bags (שקית נייר, *sakit neyar*) are sold in packaging and kitchen shops and often given by bakeries; a clean cotton cloth bag works too.
- **Fridge and freezer.** Keep the fridge for dough, not bread (it stales fastest there); check the freezer reads −18 °C before you rely on it for three months.
- **Home oven and the base.** If your bread's base burns (pain ferré) in a strong bottom-heat oven, bake one shelf higher or slide a second tray under the first.

### Steps

1. **Bake.** Make, shape and proof six bâtards of 270 g as in lesson [07.3](lesson-03.md); bake them in two loads at 240 °C with your best steam setup, about 25 minutes. Bake the test roll with the second load.
2. **Défournement.** Weigh each bâtard straight out of the oven. Probe the core of one: at least about 90 °C. Listen to the crust as it cools.
3. **Ressuage.** Cool all on a rack for 2 hours. Weigh again. Cut nothing before that.
4. **Store, labelled with date and time:**
   - bâtards 1-2: paper bag, room temperature;
   - bâtards 3-4: sealed plastic bag, room temperature;
   - bâtards 5-6: sealed plastic bag, fridge;
   - test roll: sealed in plastic, freezer.
5. **Day 1 (24 h):** take bâtards 1, 3 and 5. Weigh. Judge the crust (crisp, leathery, soft), press the cut crumb with a finger (springs back, stays dented, crumbles), smell and taste. Write a score from 1 to 5 for crust and for crumb.
6. **Day 2 (48 h):** the same with bâtards 2, 4 and 6. Look for mould on every one. Thaw the test roll 1 hour, refresh it 5 minutes in a 180 °C oven, cool 10 minutes and judge it the same way.
7. **Refresh test:** put half of the stalest bâtard in a hot oven for 5 minutes, cool 10 minutes and compare with the other half.
8. **Write two lines** for a customer: how to keep this bread and for how long.

### Targets

- Baking loss from pâton to cooled bread calculated for each bâtard (expect roughly 15-22 % for this size).
- Core at least about 90 °C at the end of baking.
- Ressuage of 2 hours before any cutting or bagging.
- A table of crust and crumb scores for each method at 24 h and 48 h, with mould noted.

### How you know it worked

Your table shows the two kinds of staling separately: the paper-bag bread keeps its crust but its crumb firms; the plastic-bag bread keeps a softer crumb but its crust goes leathery; the fridge bread has the firmest crumb of all; the frozen and refreshed roll is closest to fresh. The refreshed half tastes fresher than the other half for a short while. If your results differ, your notes on room temperature and humidity should explain why.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix now | Prevent next time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pain plat: flat, spread loaf | Over-proofed; weak or soft dough; loose shaping; oven too cool | — | Shorter or cooler apprêt; strength at pointage and shaping; preheat fully |
| Pain cloqué: blistered crust | Pieces wet from a too humid proof or condensation; too much steam; long cold retarding | — | Lower cabinet humidity; let retarded pieces lose their condensation; less steam |
| Pain cintré: burst along the side | Under-proofed; too little steam; cuts too shallow; top heat too strong | — | Full apprêt; steam at loading; cut through the skin |
| Pain ferré: burnt, hard base | Sole too hot; bread too long on the sole | Next load on a net or double tray | Check the sole temperature; lower the bottom heat |
| Croûte terne: dull crust | No steam; skinned dough; over-fermented (sugars used up); oven too cool | Longer bake for colour | Steam; cover pieces; judge fermentation by the dough |
| Gummy crumb | Under-baked; cut while warm | Return to the oven if just out | Core at least about 90 °C; full ressuage before cutting |
| Soft crust, mould after a day | Bagged warm; stored in plastic in a warm, humid room | Unbag, dry on a rack | Bag only when cold; paper for crusty bread; freeze what will not be eaten |

## Review

- In the oven: oven spring to about 46-50 °C, starch gelatinises from 55-60 °C, gluten sets at 70-80 °C, the core stays under 100 °C, the dry crust colours from roughly 150 °C.
- Bigger, denser or richer means lower temperature and longer bake; check colour, sound, weight and core at défournement.
- Ressuage on racks before any cutting or bagging; semi-finished products dated, filmed and stored in their place.
- Staling = crumb firming (starch retrogradation, fastest in the fridge) + crust softening (water moving out to the crust); freeze, refresh, never refrigerate bread.
- Exam-relevant (S3.1.6, C2.4, oven types): see [the CAP exam reference](../../references/cap-exam.md).
