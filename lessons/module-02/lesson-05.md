# 02.5 · Salt

Salt is under 2 % of the flour weight, yet bread without it is pale in taste, sticky to work and ferments out of control. This lesson explains what salt does to dough and bread, the French salt-reduction agreement every baker now works with, and ends with you baking a salted and an unsalted dough side by side.

## Why it matters

Salt is the ingredient you are most likely to forget, double, or add at the wrong time, and each mistake shows in the bread. It is also a public-health issue: bread is one of the main sources of salt in the French diet, so the bakery sector signed a national agreement to lower it. A CAP baker must be able to explain why a dose is chosen, both technically and nutritionally (référentiel S2.1.5).

## Key terms

| French | Say it | English meaning |
|---|---|---|
| sel | *SELL* | salt (sodium chloride); "sel de cuisine" in the bread decree |
| sel fin | *sell FAN* | fine salt, the usual bakery salt: dissolves fast |
| salage | *sah-LAHZH* | salting: when and how salt goes into the dough |
| salage différé | *sah-LAHZH dee-fay-RAY* | delayed salting: adding salt late in mixing |
| hygroscopique | *ee-gros-ko-PEEK* | hygroscopic: attracts water from the air |
| pression osmotique | *pres-YOHN oz-mo-TEEK* | osmotic pressure: salt pulls water out of cells (yeast, bacteria) |
| fade | *FAHD* | bland, flat-tasting |
| accord collectif | *ah-KOR ko-lek-TEEF* | collective agreement between the state and a sector |

## How it works

### What salt does

| Effect | Mechanism | In practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Flavour** | salt taste and it lifts the wheat and fermentation aromas | bread without salt tastes flat (*fade*) |
| **Tightens gluten** | salt ions shield charges on the gluten proteins so they bond more tightly | dough is firmer, more elastic, less sticky; holds gas better (more volume) |
| **Slows fermentation** | osmotic pressure pulls water out of yeast cells and slows them | regular, controllable rise; without salt the dough rushes and goes slack |
| **Crust colour** | slower fermentation leaves more sugars for the Maillard reaction and caramelisation | better-coloured crust |
| **Keeping** | hygroscopic: holds water | crumb keeps moister; in a humid room the crust softens faster |
| **Limits oxidation** | salt added at the start of mixing slows the oxidation of flour pigments | creamier crumb and more flavour than with delayed salting |

The gluten-tightening effect is why a dough mixed without salt and then salted at the end suddenly "firms up" in the mixer. French bakers usually add salt at the start or within the first minutes of slow mixing. **Delayed salting** (*salage différé*) lets the dough take more oxygen, giving a whiter crumb and more volume but less flavour; it is a technique to know, not the default.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Never leave salt and fresh yeast in direct contact (same pot, same corner of the bowl) before mixing. Salt pulls water out of the yeast cells and damages them. Put them apart on the flour or dissolve the yeast in the water first.

### How much salt: technique and nutrition

Technically, bread works between about 1.5 % and 2.2 % of the flour weight. The national agreement now sets the upper limit in practice.

**The French bread salt agreement.** On 3 March 2022, the French Directorate-General for Food (DGAL) and the Deputy Director-General for Health signed a voluntary collective agreement with the bakery sector, under the national food and nutrition programme (PNAN 2019–2023), to lower salt in bread by about 10 % in under four years. The thresholds, in grams of salt per 100 g of **bread** (not of flour), as published by the Ministry of Agriculture:

| Bread | From July 2022 | From October 2023 | From October 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pains courants (e.g. baguette) | 1.5 g | 1.4 g | — |
| Wholemeal or cereal breads | — | 1.3 g | — |
| Pain de mie | — | 1.2 g | 1.1 g |

So today a baguette should contain at most **1.4 g of salt per 100 g** and a pain de mie at most **1.1 g per 100 g**. France has also committed to the World Health Organization to cut total salt intake by 30 % by 2025, and the national nutrition programme (PNNS) asks food professionals to limit salt, sugar and fat (lesson [02.11](lesson-11.md)).

### From % of flour to g per 100 g of bread

The agreement is about the baked bread, but your formula is in % of flour. Between the two come the water, the other ingredients and the baking loss. Per 100 g of flour in a pain courant:

- dough ≈ 100 flour + 65 water + 1.8 salt + 1.5 yeast ≈ 168 g;
- baking loss for baguettes about 20 % → bread ≈ 168 × 0.80 ≈ 135 g;
- salt in bread = 1.8 ÷ 135 × 100 ≈ **1.33 g per 100 g** → below 1.4 g.

At 2.0 % of flour the same calculation gives about 1.48 g per 100 g: above the threshold. That is why the course's [base formulas](../../references/formulas.md) use 1.8–2.0 % and recommend about 1.8 % for baguettes.

## Worked example

You mix 12 kg of T55 flour for baguettes at 65 % hydration, 1.5 % fresh yeast, and want to respect the 1.4 g/100 g threshold with a margin.

1. Choose 1.8 % salt: 12,000 × 1.8 / 100 = **216 g** of salt.
2. Dough weight ≈ 12,000 + 7,800 water + 216 salt + 180 yeast = 20,196 g.
3. Bread weight after a 20 % baking loss ≈ 20,196 × 0.80 ≈ 16,157 g.
4. Salt per 100 g bread = 216 ÷ 16,157 × 100 ≈ **1.34 g**. Conforms.
5. Check the common slip: if a trainee reads "1.8 %" as 1.8 % of the dough weight, they weigh 20,196 × 1.8 / 100 ≈ 364 g: 1.7 times too much (about 2.25 g per 100 g of bread). Always calculate from the flour.

If the bread is baked smaller (more crust, more loss, say 22 %), salt per 100 g rises slightly: bread weight falls, salt stays the same. Small pieces such as rolls need checking too.

## Practice

> [!WARNING]
> Hot oven at 240 °C with steam: oven gloves, preheated metal tray for steam on the lowest shelf, pour water standing back, never into a hot glass dish.

### You need

- Minimum: scale (0.1 g pocket scale ideal for salt), probe thermometer, two bowls, two marked clear containers, scraper, baking tray, metal tray for steam, oven gloves, lame or sharp knife, [bake log](../../templates/bake-log.md).
- Professional equivalent: salt weighed on the 1 g scale per batch, checked off on the production sheet.

### Ingredients

| Ingredient | Dough S (salted) | Dough N (no salt) | Baker's % |
|---|---|---|---|
| T55 flour | 250 g | 250 g | 100 |
| Water | 163 g | 163 g | 65 |
| Salt | 4.5 g | 0 g | 1.8 / 0 |
| Fresh yeast (or instant dry 1.3 g) | 3.8 g | 3.8 g | 1.5 |

### Steps

1. Calculate water temperature for a 24 °C dough ([Module 5](../module-05/lesson-02.md) rule of thumb for hand mixing: water ≈ 70 − flour temp − room temp).
2. Mix both doughs the same way: yeast dissolved in water, flour added, rest 10 minutes, salt added to dough S only, knead 8 minutes.
3. Note right away: which is stickier? Which feels firmer and springs back more?
4. Put each in a marked clear container. Record the time each takes to double in volume.
5. Divide each into two 200 g pieces, shape into balls, proof 45–60 minutes.
6. Bake at 240 °C with steam for 20–22 minutes. Cool 1 hour.
7. Compare: volume, crust colour, crumb, and taste (taste N first, then S).
8. Bonus calculation: weigh one cooled loaf of S and calculate its salt per 100 g of bread.

### Targets

- Dough temperatures 23–25 °C, both within 1 °C of each other.
- Salt weighed to 0.1 g (or to 1 g with a kitchen scale; note it).
- Salt per 100 g of bread for dough S: about 1.3–1.4 g.

### How you know it worked

Dough N is stickier, slacker and rises faster (often 15–30 % quicker to double). Its bread is flatter, with a paler crust and a flat taste. Dough S is firmer, rises steadily and gives better volume, a golden crust and full flavour.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix now | Prevent next time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dough sticky, rises very fast, bread flat and bland | Salt forgotten | If still mixing, add salt and mix 2–3 min more; if already fermenting, report it — the batch may need to be sold as non-conforming or used otherwise | Weigh salt first and tick it off on the production sheet |
| Very slow rise, tight dough, salty taste | Salt doubled or weighed as % of dough | Lengthen fermentation; report the non-conformity; do not sell over the salt threshold | Always calculate % of flour; second person checks the salt pot |
| Patches of poor rise in the dough | Salt and yeast left in contact, or salt not dissolved | — | Separate salt and yeast; use fine salt |
| Crust softens quickly in the shop | Hygroscopic salt plus humid air; under-baked | Re-bake briefly if allowed | Bake well; display away from steam and humidity |
| Very white crumb, little flavour | Salt added very late (salage différé) and long intensive mixing | — | Add salt early in mixing for traditional flavour |

## Review

- Salt flavours, tightens gluten, slows and regulates fermentation, helps crust colour and keeps the crumb moist.
- Usual dose about 1.8–2.0 % of flour; calculate from the flour, never from the dough.
- Agreement of 3 March 2022: pains courants 1.4 g of salt per 100 g of bread since October 2023; wholemeal/cereal breads 1.3 g; pain de mie 1.1 g since October 2025.
- Keep salt and fresh yeast apart before mixing; add salt early unless the formula says otherwise.
