# 18.2 · Waste, Food Waste and Unsold Bread

Every evening a bakery has unsold bread, and every day it fills bins with flour sacks, cardboard, film, dough scraps and eggshells. This lesson gives the legal order of priority against food waste, the sorting rules that apply to a small bakery today, and what may and may not be done with yesterday's bread, from chapelure to donation. You keep a one-week waste log and turn stale bread into three products on the falling heat of your oven.

## Why it matters

The référentiel asks you to apply the bakery's sorting rules and waste instructions (C2.7), to justify selective sorting (S4.3) and to name sustainable-development actions in the company, including the management of raw materials and waste (S1.1); see [The CAP Boulanger Exam](../../references/cap-exam.md). The money is real too. An ADEME study of 19 craft bakeries (2023, reported by the CNBPF) found a median food waste of 1,204 kg a year, **1.9 % of turnover**, with bread the most wasted product by weight; INRAE puts unsold bread at about 10 % of a craft bakery's production. Each baguette thrown away costs at least its production cost, about €0.60 in lesson [06.5](../module-06/lesson-05.md).

## How it works

### The legal order of priority

The loi Garot of 11 February 2016 set the order in which food waste must be fought; later laws (EGalim 2018, the 2019 ordinance, AGEC 2020) extended it (Ministry of Agriculture, page of 28 September 2026):

![Inverted pyramid of the food-waste hierarchy with bakery examples: 1 prevent (size loads from unsold counts, last load smaller, FIFO), 2 use for people (reduced-price baskets, chapelure, croûtons, pain perdu, donation), 3 animal feed, 4 compost or methanisation through the biowaste collection, 5 disposal as residual waste only for what is left; packaging sorted in the separate streams](../../assets/m18-waste-hierarchy.svg)

1. **Prevention:** do not produce what will not sell.
2. **Use of unsold food fit for people**, by **donation** or by **processing** it into another food.
3. **Animal feed.**
4. **Composting** for agriculture or **energy recovery** by methanisation.

What the laws oblige depends on the business (Ministry of Agriculture):

| Who | Duty |
|---|---|
| Food manufacturers, wholesalers, distributors, collective catering | must not make edible unsold food unfit for consumption (no bleach on the bin) |
| Food distributors over **400 m²** | must offer a donation agreement to an approved food-aid association; donation quality plan |
| Collective catering over 3,000 meals a day, food manufacturers and wholesalers over €50 M turnover | donation agreements; waste diagnosis |
| Commercial restaurants | must offer a take-away box for leftovers since 1 July 2021 |

A **craft bakery is not in these categories**: it has no legal duty to sign a donation agreement. The hierarchy is still the method the trade applies (the CNBPF guide sets it out for bakers), donation is open to any bakery, and the waste-sorting rules below apply to all.

### Sorting rules that do apply to a small bakery

- **Biowaste: every professional since 1 January 2024.** Sorting biowaste at source, once only for large producers (over 120 t a year in 2012, then 10 t and 5 t), now applies to all professionals and households with **no minimum quantity** (Ministry of Ecological Transition). In a bakery that means unsold bread and viennoiserie not used otherwise, dough scraps, eggshells, coffee grounds: kept out of the residual bin and sent to a biowaste collection or composting.
- **Packaging and other dry waste: the "6 streams".** Professionals must sort paper/cardboard, metal, plastic, glass, wood and, since 1 January 2025, textiles, and have them collected separately. A professional who uses the municipal collection and produces **under 1,100 litres of waste a week** (all waste together) follows the local sorting rules of the commune instead, but still sorts (DGPR FAQ, 2024). Most small bakeries are in that case: flour sacks and cardboard to paper recycling, film and plastic tubs as the commune says.
- **Hazardous waste** (empty cleaning-product containers marked with danger pictograms, oils) follows the instructions on the label and the house rules (lesson [18.3](lesson-03.md)).

### Preventing unsold bread

Prevention is the cheapest level. The CNBPF guide's first advice is to **weigh** waste for a starting diagnosis, then:

- **size the loads from the unsold counts** (lesson [15.5](../module-15/lesson-05.md)): if 15 baguettes are left every evening, the last load is too big;
- bake the late loads smaller and more often, and take orders for large quantities;
- manage stock first-in, first-out (FIFO) for raw materials;
- sell yesterday's suitable products at a reduced price, for example in an anti-waste basket, labelled as such.

### Reusing unsold bread: what is allowed

| Reuse | How | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| **Chapelure** | dry the bread, grind it, dry again at about 150 °C (King Arthur: 5-15 min) | very dark crust gives bitter crumbs (and, at research level, raises acrylamide questions: INRAE); store dry, labelled and dated |
| **Croûtons** | cubes with a little oil and salt, about 175 °C for 7-10 min | for the snack counter or salads; same labelling |
| **Pain perdu, bread pudding** | soaked in milk, egg and sugar, then baked or pan-cooked | new product: new allergens (egg, milk), new storage rules |
| **Old bread in new dough** (pain courant) | dried crumb or soaked bread weighed into a pain courant formula as a house practice, counting its water and salt | see below |
| **Donation** | to an association that collects the same evening | food safety of what is given; nothing spoiled or unsafe |
| **Animal feed** | through a farmer or channel allowed by the rules for animal feed | ask the house; not everything that is unfit for people is fit for animals |

**Old bread in new dough.** In **pain courant** there is no rule against it: bakers recycle dried or soaked bread into a new dough as a house practice, and INRAE's µ-COSMOS project studies exactly this, noting that stale-bread powders bring **gelatinised starch and denatured gluten** (they absorb water but build no gluten network) and that the dark crust is the problem part. Treat it as a formula change: start small (this course suggests trying 5 % of the flour weight as dry crumb), count the crumb in the dough weight and the soaking water inside the formula's hydration, and judge the result with the quality rubric.

In **pain de tradition française**, the answer is **no**. Article 2 of décret 93-1074 says the dough is composed **exclusively** of bread-making wheat flours, drinking water and salt, fermented with yeast and/or levain, with only small amounts of bean, soy and malt flour. Bread is none of these, so old bread in the dough makes the loaf a pain courant, whatever it was before (lesson [09.1](../module-09/lesson-01.md)). For the same reason a tradition pâte fermentée must itself be tradition dough.

Two rules apply to every reuse:

- **Allergens follow the bread.** Crumbs from a seeded loaf bring sesame; crumbs from pain viennois or brioche bring milk and egg. Reuse a bread only in a product whose written allergen information covers what it brings (lesson [02.11](../module-02/lesson-11.md)), or keep the reuse streams separate.
- **Only safe bread is reused.** Mould, bread that has been on the floor or returned by a customer, or bread kept in bad conditions goes to biowaste, never into food or a donation. The hygiene rules for reuse and donation (storage, dating, transport) are Module 17's subject.

Freezing unsold traditions to sell them the next day remains forbidden in a boulangerie (lesson [09.1](../module-09/lesson-01.md)).

### Donation and its tax treatment

A bakery may give unsold bread and viennoiserie to a food-aid association. Gifts to organisations that provide free meals to people in difficulty give a **60 % tax reduction**, the gift in kind being valued at its **cost (coût de revient)**, not its selling price, within a ceiling of €20,000 or 0.5 % of turnover a year, whichever is higher; excess carries forward 5 years (Entreprendre.service-public, verified July 2026). The accountant claims it; the baker keeps a dated record of what was given.

## Worked example

Boulangerie du Marché keeps a waste log for one week (6 opening days). Production cost of a baguette from lesson [06.5](../module-06/lesson-05.md): €0.60.

| Day | Baguettes made | Unsold baguettes | Unsold traditions | Dough scraps (g) | Cardboard and flour sacks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tue-Sun (total) | 2,400 | 180 | 42 | 2,100 | 3 bags |

1. **Cost of the unsold baguettes.** 180 × €0.60 = **€108 a week**; over 50 working weeks about **€5,400 a year**, before counting the traditions.
2. **Diagnosis.** 180 ÷ 6 = 30 a day, 7.5 % of the baguettes made, and the log shows 24 of the 30 come from the 16:00 load.
3. **Prevent first.** The 16:00 load goes from 60 to 40 baguettes, with a small 17:30 load of 12 if the shelf empties (15.5's rule). Expected unsold: about 10 a day.
4. **Use for people.** The remaining plain baguettes become chapelure and croûtons for the snack counter (dried and toasted on the falling heat, lesson [18.1](lesson-01.md)) and 5 % dry crumb in the next day's pain courant. The traditions: sold in the evening anti-waste basket or donated; **not** put into tradition dough.
5. **Donation.** An association collects 10 items each evening. Cost value about 10 × €0.60 = €6 a day; over 300 days about €1,800, giving about €1,080 of tax reduction (60 %), well under the ceiling.
6. **The rest.** Dough scraps, eggshells and spoiled bread go to the biowaste bin (sorting compulsory since 1 January 2024); flour sacks (shaken out) and flattened cardboard to the paper stream; film to the stream the commune sets, since the shop produces well under 1,100 L of waste a week.
7. **Report.** A one-page summary for the owner: cost per week before and after, what changed, who does what, and a target (unsold under 3 % of baguettes made within a month).

## Practice

You keep a one-week waste log of your own kitchen's bread and baking, then turn stale bread into chapelure, croûtons and pain perdu using the oven's falling heat.

> [!CAUTION]
> Pain perdu contains raw egg and milk: wash your hands and the bowl after handling raw egg, cook it through (no runny centre) and remember both are allergens for anyone you serve. Cut stale bread on a board, knife moving away from you; the oven is at 175-250 °C, so use dry oven gloves.

### You need

- Kitchen scale, a bag or box per waste stream for the week, a notebook or a copy of the log below, an oven thermometer, baking tray, bowl, frying pan or baking dish, a blender or a grater for crumbs, an airtight jar with a label.
- Professional equivalent: the bakery's waste log and sales sheet, the biowaste bin and its collection, the paper and cardboard stream, the donation register.

### Ingredients

| Item | Quantity | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Stale plain bread (no mould) | about 500 g, kept separate by type | 150 g chapelure, 150 g croûtons, 200 g pain perdu |
| Oil or melted butter | 15 g | croûtons |
| Salt | 1-2 g | croûtons |
| Eggs | 2 (about 100 g) | pain perdu |
| Milk | 200 g | pain perdu |
| Sugar | 20 g | pain perdu |
| Butter | 10 g | pan or dish |

### In Israel

Checked 2026-10-09.

- **What goes stale at home:** the end of the Friday challah, pita and sliced bread. Plain bread and challah do not carry the same allergens (challah usually contains egg): keep them apart in your log and in your reuse, as a bakery would. Breadcrumbs are *pirurei lechem* (פירורי לחם), croutons *krutonim* (קרוטונים); pain perdu made with challah is the classic Israeli French toast.
- **Sorting:** follow your municipality's bin guide. In Tel Aviv-Yafo, for example: blue bins take paper and thin cardboard (a shaken-out paper flour bag), orange bins take clean, empty packaging (plastic bags from yeast or seeds, tubs) but no food scraps, grey-purple bins take glass, deposit bottles go back to the shop, and food scraps except meat and dairy go to a home composter. Other towns use different colours and streams: check yours.
- **Donation:** surplus food in Israel is collected by food-rescue organisations such as Leket Israel; a home baker practising the hierarchy will mostly stop at levels 1 and 2 (bake less, reuse).

### Steps

1. **Set up the log** (7 days): date; item (bread type, dough scrap, viennoiserie, other food); weight (g); reason (stale, mould, burnt, over-produced, scraps); what you did with it (eaten, reused, composted or biowaste, residual bin); packaging items by stream.
2. **Weigh and record everything** bread-related you would normally throw away, every day. Mouldy bread goes straight to biowaste or compost after weighing: it is never reused.
3. **On day 7, bake the reuse trio on one preheat, hottest first:** if you bake bread that day, start with it; then turn the oven to about 175 °C for the croûtons (cubes of about 1.5 cm, oil and salt, 7-10 min, tossed halfway); then pain perdu in a buttered dish (bread soaked 5 minutes in the egg, milk and sugar mix) at about 175 °C until set and golden, about 20-25 minutes; finally switch off and dry the chapelure (blended or grated stale bread on a tray) in the falling heat, checking every 5 minutes.
4. **Label** the jar of chapelure: product, bread it came from, date made. Store dry.
5. **Analyse the week:** total weight wasted, share by reason, share by destination, and the cost (use the price you paid per kg of bread or your ingredient cost).
6. **Write your reuse plan:** three changes, one per level of the hierarchy you can act on (for example: bake 600 g of flour instead of 1 kg on weekdays; freeze half a loaf the day you bake it; turn ends into chapelure every Friday).
7. **Optional trial:** in your next pain courant-style dough (e.g. PD-01 with 500 g flour), add 25 g of your dry chapelure (5 % of the flour), soaked in 25 g of the formula's water, and compare the crumb and keeping with your usual loaf. Never in a dough you are practising as tradition.

### Targets

- Seven days logged, every item weighed to the gram, every item given a reason and a destination.
- Chapelure dry and crisp (snaps, no soft lumps), croûtons golden and crisp, pain perdu set in the centre.
- A reuse plan with one action at each level you can reach, and a target for next week (e.g. half the weight).

### How you know it worked

Your log shows where your waste comes from (often the same item, such as the second half of a loaf), and the plan attacks that source first. Week two weighs less. For example, a log of 1,240 g of bread in a week, 70 % of it "stale ends of loaves", leads to baking smaller loaves or freezing half on the day; reusing the rest as chapelure and croûtons takes the bread in the bin close to zero, with only mouldy or spoiled bread going to compost.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix now | Prevent next time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Many unsold baguettes every evening | Last load sized by habit | Record the count; reuse or donate the edible ones | Size loads from the unsold counts; smaller late loads |
| Chapelure bitter and dark | Crust very dark or burnt bread used | Sieve out dark pieces; use for coatings only after tasting | Use plain, well-baked bread; dry at moderate heat |
| Chapelure mouldy in the jar | Not fully dry; stored warm | Throw it away (biowaste) | Dry until it snaps; cool fully before closing the jar |
| A "tradition" made with yesterday's bread in the dough | Decree not checked | Sell it as pain courant, not tradition | Old bread only in pain courant; check art. 2 |
| Customer reacts to sesame in croûtons | Crumbs from seeded bread reused without updating allergens | Withdraw; inform the customer; report | Separate reuse streams; allergen sheet updated for every reuse |
| Bread scraps in the residual bin | Biowaste sorting not organised | Move them to the biowaste bin | Biowaste bin at the bench; sorting on the house sheet |

## Review

- Hierarchy (loi Garot 2016): prevent, use for people (donation, processing), animal feed, compost or methanisation; disposal only for the rest.
- A craft bakery has no donation-agreement duty (that is for stores over 400 m² and large operators), but every professional must sort biowaste since 1 January 2024 and sort paper, metal, plastic, glass, wood and textiles, following the commune's rules under 1,100 L a week.
- Prevent with the unsold counts; reuse as chapelure, croûtons, pain perdu, or crumb in pain courant; never old bread in tradition dough; allergens follow the bread; spoiled bread is never reused (hygiene: Module 17).
- Donation at cost gives a 60 % tax reduction within a ceiling; keep a record.
- Exam-relevant (C2.7 sorting, S1.1 sustainable development, S4.3 waste): see [the CAP exam reference](../../references/cap-exam.md).
