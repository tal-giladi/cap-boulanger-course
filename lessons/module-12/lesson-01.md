# 12.1 · Pain au Chocolat: What Changes from Croissant

A pain au chocolat is made from exactly the same laminated dough as a croissant: same détrempe, same butter, same turns. What changes is everything after the last turn: the sheet is left a little thicker, it is cut into rectangles instead of triangles, it is rolled around two chocolate batons instead of being stretched and rolled into a crescent, and it proofs and bakes seam underneath. This lesson explains each of those changes and why it exists, and you make pains au chocolat and croissants from one home lamination.

## Why it matters

Croissants, pains au chocolat and pains aux raisins come from one pâte levée feuilletée, and the référentiel lists all three together under C2.3 and S3.4; the production test asks for them from one batch of dough (what and how many: [The CAP Boulanger Exam](../../references/cap-exam.md)). In a bakery the three share one lamination every morning, so the tourier must cut a sheet into several products without waste and without changing the method halfway. A pain au chocolat that unrolls in the oven, shows its chocolate on top or bakes flat is not a croissant fault: it is a cutting or rolling fault that belongs to this product.

## How it works

### Same dough, different finish

Everything up to the last turn is lessons [11.2](../module-11/lesson-02.md) to [11.4](../module-11/lesson-04.md): a cool détrempe, butter at about 13 °C (12-15 °C) so it bends without breaking (King Arthur Baking), three single turns (tours simples), or one double and one single in a warm kitchen, with rests in the cold. The dough is the course croissant sheet **CR-01** of lesson 11.2, inside the croissant column of the [base formulas](../../references/formulas.md):

| Ingredient | Base formulas range (% of flour) | CR-01 (lesson [11.2](../module-11/lesson-02.md)) |
|---|---|---|
| T45 flour (or T45 with part gruau) | 100 | 100 |
| Water | 25-30 | 26 |
| Milk | 25-30 | 26 |
| Sugar | 10-12 | 11 |
| Salt | 2.0 | 2.0 |
| Fresh yeast | 3.5-4.5 | 4 |
| Butter in the détrempe | 5-10 | 8 |
| **Détrempe total** | | **177** |
| Butter for lamination (beurrage) | 45-55 | 50 |
| **Laminated dough total** | | **227** |

Batch rule from lesson [11.2](../module-11/lesson-02.md): laminated dough = pieces ÷ 0.90 (10 % trimmings) × 1.02 (process losses); flour = laminated dough ÷ 2.27.

### What changes after the last turn

| Step | Croissant ([Module 11](../module-11/lesson-05.md)) | Pain au chocolat | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Final sheet | about 3.5 mm | about 4 mm | the pain au chocolat is rolled, not stretched; a slightly thicker sheet gives it the same height |
| Cut | long triangles | rectangles about 8.5 cm × 15 cm | the 8.5 cm side is the length of a baton plus a few millimetres, so both ends of the chocolate are covered |
| Piece weight | about 60 g (lesson [11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md)) | about 58-60 g of dough + 2 batons (about 10.5 g) | weigh the first rectangle and adjust the length, not the width |
| Shaping | stretched, rolled from the base, curved or straight | not stretched; rolled around two batons | stretching a rectangle makes it narrower than the batons |
| Seam | tip underneath | last edge underneath, in the middle of the base | the weight of the piece holds the roll closed during proof and baking |
| Proof and egg wash | 24-26 °C, 75-80 % humidity, below about 27 °C, about 1 h 30-2 h 30; egg wash before baking | the same | butter must stay solid (King Arthur Baking) |
| Bake | 190-200 °C conventional (175-180 °C fan), no steam | the same oven, one or two minutes longer | slightly thicker piece |

### Rolling around two batons

1. Lay the rectangle with a short side (8.5 cm) towards you. Place the first baton about 1-1.5 cm from the near edge, parallel to it.
2. Fold the near edge over the baton and press lightly so the dough grips.
3. Place the second baton against the fold, on the dough.
4. Roll on, without pulling, to the far edge. The far edge ends **underneath, in the middle** of the piece; press the roll very lightly with the flat of the hand to make it slightly rectangular.

Placed this way, each baton has dough on every side, the two batons are about 2.5-3 cm apart, and a cut piece shows two dark eyes in a spiral of layers. If the seam ends on the side, the piece opens in the oven as the layers lift.

### Why the batons do not melt

Baking chocolate batons are made with **low fluidity** (less cocoa butter) so they soften inside the piece but keep their shape and do not run out into the layers (Callebaut). A common professional baton is about 8 cm long with at least 44 % cocoa and weighs about 5.3 g (300 sticks in a 1.6 kg box; Cacao Barry). They are stored cool and dry, around 17-18 °C, away from smells and humidity, not in the fridge, where they take up moisture. A bar of eating chocolate cut into sticks melts much more and often leaks.

### Rests and the sheet

A sheet that has just been rolled is elastic: cut at once, the rectangles shrink and the layers distort. Rest the final sheet in the cold (about 10-20 minutes, covered) before cutting, and cut with a straight, sharp, downward press of the knife or a rolling cutter: dragging the blade or sawing seals the layers at the cut edge, and they cannot open in the oven. The same rule applies to croissants (lesson [11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md)).

> [!WARNING]
> Cut away from your other hand with a sharp knife or pastry wheel, keep the fingers of the guiding hand curled, and put the knife down flat, never into the sink full of water. In a bakery the final sheet is thinned on the laminoir: hands stay away from the rollers and its guard stays closed while it runs.

## Worked example

Friday, Boulangerie du Marché. Saturday's order from the shop: **48 croissants and 48 pains au chocolat**, from one lamination. Croissant pieces 60 g; pain au chocolat 58 g of dough + 2 batons. Sheet CR-01 (laminated dough 227 % of flour); the batch rule of lesson [11.2](../module-11/lesson-02.md): pieces ÷ 0.90 for 10 % trimmings, × 1.02 for process losses. Flour rounded up to the next 100 g.

1. **Laminated dough needed.** 48 × 60 + 48 × 58 = 2,880 + 2,784 = 5,664 g of pieces. 5,664 ÷ 0.90 × 1.02 = 6,419 g.
2. **Flour.** 6,419 ÷ 2.27 = 2,828 g → **2,900 g**. (The common slip, dividing by the détrempe total 1.77, gives 3,627 g: about 800 g of flour too much, because the beurrage is then forgotten.)
3. **Détrempe.** Water 754 g, milk 754 g, sugar 319 g, salt 58 g, fresh yeast 116 g, butter 232 g; détrempe 5,133 g. **Beurrage:** 50 % of 2,900 g = **1,450 g** of beurre de tourage. Laminated dough 6,583 g ≥ 6,419 g.
4. **Batons.** 48 × 2 = **96 batons**, about 96 × 5.3 = 509 g: one 1.6 kg box (about 300) covers three such orders.
5. **Dividing the sheet.** Croissants and pains au chocolat need different sheet thicknesses, so the tourier cuts the laminated block in two after the last turn: about 3,260 g for croissants and about 3,160 g for pains au chocolat (each need ÷ 0.90 × 1.02), and gives each its own final pass through the laminoir.
6. **Pain au chocolat band.** At 4 mm the band is cut 15 cm wide; rectangles every 8.5 cm along it. The first rectangle weighs 62 g: the tourier cuts the band to 14.5 cm (about 3 % less) and the next one weighs 59 g.
7. **Check after shaping.** 48 pieces on 4 trays of 12, seam underneath, staggered so they do not touch when they double. Cabinet at 25 °C, 75-80 % humidity.

## Practice

You laminate a 500 g-flour batch of CR-01 as in [Module 11](../module-11/lesson-02.md), then shape **8 pains au chocolat and about 6 croissants** from it and bake them side by side.

> [!WARNING]
> The oven is at about 190-200 °C: dry oven gloves, open the door and stand back from the hot air. Cut with a sharp knife or pastry wheel away from your fingers. If you use a mixer for the détrempe, never put a hand or scraper in the bowl while the hook turns.

> [!CAUTION]
> Egg wash is raw egg: make it fresh, keep it in the fridge, wash your hands after cracking eggs and discard what is left. These pastries contain wheat (gluten), milk and egg, and the chocolate usually contains soy (lecithin) and sometimes milk: read its label before you share them.

### You need

- Minimum: scale (1 g), probe thermometer, rolling pin, two 4 mm guides (two wooden strips or thick rulers laid either side of the dough so the pin cannot go thinner), ruler, sharp knife or pastry wheel, pastry brush, two trays with baking paper, cling film, wire rack, [bake log](../../templates/bake-log.md).
- Professional equivalent: laminoir (sheeter), roller cutter set to 8.5 cm, proofing cabinet at 24-26 °C and 75-80 % humidity, rack or deck oven.

### In Israel

Checked 2026-10-08.

- **Chocolate batons** (*maklot shokolad le'afiya*, מקלות שוקולד לאפייה, "baking chocolate sticks"): look in baking-supply shops and their online stores. On the label look for "bake stable" or "stable in baking" (*amid be'afiya*, עמיד באפייה), a length of about 8 cm, and 40-55 % cocoa. Not found? Cut a 50-60 % dark baking chocolate bar into sticks of about 8 × 1 cm (about 5 g): it works, but softens more and may leak; keep the sticks in the fridge until the last moment.
- **Kashrut and allergens:** chocolate is labelled dairy (*khalavi*, חלבי) or pareve (*parve*, פרווה); with butter and milk in the dough the pastry is dairy anyway. Read the allergen line (*alergenim*, אלרגנים) for soy and milk.
- **Butter:** buy block butter (*khem'a*, חמאה), unsalted, and read the fat line on the label: look for about 80-82 g of fat per 100 g, the level of French butter (lesson [02.8](../module-02/lesson-08.md)). Israeli Standard SI 323 covers butter made from cream; half-fat butter (*khem'a khatzi-shmena*, חמאה חצי-שמנה) and spreads (*mimrach*, ממרח) are outside it and will not laminate.
- **Flour:** white flour (*kemakh lavan*, קמח לבן) in place of T45 ([Flour in Israel](../../references/flour-in-israel.md)). It is often stronger than T45: if the sheet springs back when you roll it to 4 mm, rest it 10 minutes more in the fridge rather than forcing it.
- **A 26-32 °C summer kitchen:** laminate early in the morning or in the air-conditioned room (in a warm kitchen, one double and one single turn instead of three singles, lesson [11.4](../module-11/lesson-04.md)), and put the sheet back in the fridge whenever the butter starts to shine or smear. Proofing at 30 °C melts the butter out of the layers (keep below about 27 °C): proof in the air-conditioned room at 24-26 °C, or shape in the evening, cover and leave the tray in the fridge overnight, then finish proofing in the morning (lesson [04.6](../module-04/lesson-06.md)). Fridge at 5 °C or below.

### Steps

1. **Laminate** the dough exactly as in lessons [11.2](../module-11/lesson-02.md) to [11.4](../module-11/lesson-04.md) (détrempe, beurrage, turns, rests). Keep the batons at about 17-20 °C, out of the sun.
2. **Divide the block** after the last turn: about 55 % for pains au chocolat, 45 % for croissants. Wrap the croissant part and keep it in the fridge.
3. **Roll the pain au chocolat part** to a band 15 cm wide and 4 mm thick between your guides, turning and lifting it so it does not stick. Rest it 15 minutes in the fridge, covered.
4. **Cut** 8 rectangles of 8.5 cm × 15 cm with straight, downward cuts. Weigh the first: target 58 g ± 3 g; adjust the length of the next ones if needed. Keep the trimmings separately.
5. **Roll** each rectangle around two batons (first baton 1-1.5 cm from the near edge, fold, second baton, roll on without pulling). Seam underneath in the middle; press lightly.
6. **Croissants:** roll and shape the croissant part as in lesson [11.5](../module-11/lesson-05.md).
7. **Place** the pieces on paper-lined trays, about 5 cm apart, staggered. Brush a first thin coat of egg wash on the tops only, not on the cut layers at the ends.
8. **Proof** at 24-26 °C, covered without touching (or overnight in the fridge, then at room temperature), until about doubled, the layers visible at the ends and the pieces wobbling when you shake the tray: about 1 h 30-2 h 30. Preheat the oven to 190-200 °C conventional (175-180 °C fan), no steam, with a tray inside for the last 45 minutes.
9. **Second egg wash**, thin, tops only. Bake about 16-20 minutes for pains au chocolat (one or two minutes longer than the croissants), until deep golden on top and coloured underneath.
10. **Cool** on a rack 20 minutes. Cut one pain au chocolat and one croissant across their middles and compare.

### Targets

- Sheet 4 mm; rectangles 8.5 × 15 cm; dough pieces 58 g ± 3 g, all within 6 g of each other.
- Proof below 27 °C; baked pieces about twice their shaped volume.
- Seam still underneath after baking; no chocolate visible on top or at the ends.

### How you know it worked

The pains au chocolat are straight, slightly rectangular and evenly golden, with the layers fanning out at both ends. Cut across the middle, the section shows two chocolate "eyes" fully enclosed in a spiral of thin, separate layers with an open honeycomb, no greasy grey band and no hollow under the top. Next to the croissants, the pains au chocolat are a little denser at the centre (more rolls of dough around the chocolate) but just as flaky.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix now | Prevent next time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Piece unrolled in the oven, seam on the side | Seam not underneath; rectangle stretched while rolling | Turn the next pieces seam down before proofing | Last edge underneath, in the middle; roll without pulling |
| Chocolate shows at the ends | Rectangle narrower than the batons (stretched, or cut too narrow) | Pinch the ends lightly | Width = baton length + a few mm; do not stretch |
| Chocolate ran out into the layers | Eating chocolate instead of bake-stable batons; batons kept warm | — | Bake-stable batons, stored cool |
| Pieces shrank and went oval after cutting | Sheet cut straight after rolling, still elastic | Let the pieces rest before shaping | Rest the sheet 10-20 min in the cold before cutting |
| Flat pieces, butter pool on the tray | Proofed too warm (above about 27 °C) | — | Proof at 24-26 °C or in the fridge overnight, then finish |
| Dense, doughy centre | Sheet too thick; under-proofed; under-baked | Bake a few minutes more at a lower temperature | 4 mm; full proof; colour underneath before taking out |
| Layers glued at the ends | Egg wash brushed onto the cut ends; blunt knife dragged | — | Brush tops only; sharp, straight downward cuts |

## Review

- A pain au chocolat is croissant dough with a different finish: a slightly thicker sheet (about 4 mm), rectangles about 8.5 × 15 cm, two batons, rolled without stretching, seam underneath.
- Bake-stable batons have less cocoa butter, so they hold their shape; store them cool and dry, not in the fridge.
- Rest the sheet before cutting, cut straight down, keep egg wash off the cut layers.
- Calculate the flour from the laminated-dough total (détrempe + beurrage) with an allowance for trimmings.
- The three laminated products together in the production test: see [The CAP Boulanger Exam](../../references/cap-exam.md).
