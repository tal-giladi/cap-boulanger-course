# 11.5 · Rolling, Cutting and Shaping Croissants

The laminated block becomes croissants in three movements: roll the final sheet, cut triangles, roll each one up. This lesson shows how to roll a sheet that does not shrink, how to cut regular triangles with little waste, how to hit the piece weight by adjusting the thickness, and how to roll a croissant so its layers stay open. You roll your block from lesson [11.4](lesson-04.md) and shape 16 croissants: 12 for evaluation and 4 for tests.

## Why it matters

Twelve croissants on a tray are judged as a batch: same weight, same size, same number of turns, same shape (C3.2). Irregularity here is almost always a cutting problem: a sheet thicker at one end, triangles cut by eye, some rolled tight and some loose. Cutting is also where the money goes: every gram of trim is laminated dough with butter in it. In the bakery the final sheet is rolled on the sheeter (the production test has its own rule on this: see [The CAP Boulanger Exam](../../references/cap-exam.md)); the cutting and rolling are done by hand everywhere.

## How it works

### The final sheet

- **Start cold and rested.** The block comes from its final rest (lesson [11.4](lesson-04.md)) at fridge temperature. For a home batch, cut it in two and keep one half in the fridge while you work the other: half the dough, half the time on the bench.
- **Roll gradually, in both directions**, to the thickness and size wanted, lifting the sheet now and then and letting it fall back loosely: this lets it shrink back *before* you cut, not after.
- **Rest the sheet.** If the sheet still shrinks when you lift it, slide it on a tray and put it in the fridge for 10-20 minutes (Elle & Vire rests its sheet 30-40 minutes before the last rolling). Cut a tense sheet and every triangle shortens and thickens on the tray.
- **Thickness.** CR-01 cuts 11 × 30 cm triangles from a sheet of about **3.5 mm** to make pieces of about **60 g**. Professional formulas vary: Elle & Vire rolls to 2.5 mm and cuts narrower triangles for a lighter croissant. What matters is the piece weight, so you weigh the first triangles and adjust.

### Weight, thickness and area

A triangle's weight is its area × thickness × the density of laminated dough (about 1.05 g/cm³):

$$\text{weight} \approx \frac{\text{base} \times \text{height}}{2} \times \text{thickness} \times 1.05$$

For CR-01: 11 × 30 ÷ 2 = 165 cm²; at 0.35 cm: 165 × 0.35 × 1.05 ≈ **61 g**. Since the shape is fixed, **weight is proportional to thickness**: if the first triangles weigh 55 g instead of 60 g, the sheet is 60 ÷ 55 = 1.09 times too thin. Weigh before you cut the whole sheet.

### Cutting triangles with little waste

![Strip 30 by 49.5 cm with marks every 11 cm on both edges offset by 5.5 cm, cut into 8 triangles with alternating bases and two half-triangle trims; a triangle 11 cm wide and 30 cm high with a notch in the base, stretched to about 34 cm; rolled loosely from base to tip without pressing; placed with the tip underneath, straight or as a crescent, 5 cm apart](../../assets/m11-cutting-rolling.svg)

1. **Trim the edges** straight with a long knife or a wheel: a few millimetres to 1 cm. The edges are the thinnest, least regular part, and King Arthur Baking notes that trimming them helps the croissants rise higher, because a cut edge has open layers.
2. **Cut strips** as high as the triangle (30 cm).
3. **Mark** the long edges every 11 cm (the triangle's base), the top marks offset 5.5 cm from the bottom ones. Cut from mark to mark: the triangles alternate base-down and base-up, so almost nothing is lost between them.
4. **Cut straight down**, in one firm stroke, with a sharp knife or wheel. Dragging or sawing pinches the layers together at the edge and seals them.

In the bakery a **coupe-croissants** roller cuts all the triangles of a sheet in one pass.

With a base b, a strip of length L cut this way gives (L − b/2) ÷ (b/2) full triangles, plus two half triangles at the ends (trims). For CR-01 (b = 11 cm): (L − 5.5) ÷ 5.5. A 49.5 cm strip gives 8; a 176 cm strip from the sheeter gives 31. Long strips waste a smaller share.

**Trims (chutes).** Their layers are cut across and misaligned, so they are not re-laminated into croissants. Bakeries use them by house rule (small twists, or a small share mixed into a later dough); at home, bake them as twists beside your croissants as a proof and colour test.

### Rolling up

1. **Notch** the middle of the base 1-2 cm and fold the two inner corners slightly outwards: the base widens, giving the classic wider middle and thinner ends.
2. **Stretch gently** from base to tip, holding the base and sliding a hand down to the point, to about 34 cm. A longer triangle gives more turns and a more layered croissant (King Arthur Baking).
3. **Roll loosely** from the base towards the tip, with the palms guiding, not pressing, so every croissant shows the same number of regular ridges. Pressing welds the layers together; rolling too tight leaves no room to rise and the centre stays raw.
4. **Tip underneath**, touching the tray: the weight of the croissant holds it, so it does not unroll in the oven.
5. **Shape**: leave it straight, or bend the ends towards each other for a crescent. Many French bakeries shape their butter croissants straight, so that customers can tell them apart from other croissants; this is a shop convention, not a legal rule, so follow the house sheet.
6. **On the tray** with baking paper, at least 5 cm apart (they almost double): about 6 per home tray, 12-15 per 40 × 60 cm bakery tray.

Every rolling and shaping step is done fast, with cold dough. If the sheet goes soft and sticky halfway, put the uncut part back in the fridge for 10 minutes.

## Worked example

Saturday 6:25, Boulangerie du Marché. Block 1 of CR-01 (about 4,100 g) is ready after its final rest. Order: 120 croissants of 60 g ± 3 g from two blocks.

1. **Sheet size.** On the sheeter the block is rolled to a band 62 cm wide. At 3.5 mm: length = 4,100 ÷ (62 × 0.35 × 1.05) ≈ **180 cm**.
2. **Trim** to 60 × 176 cm (1 cm off each long side, the ragged ends off): two strips of 30 × 176 cm.
3. **Triangles per strip:** (176 − 5.5) ÷ 5.5 = **31**; two strips = 62. Two blocks = 124: the 120 ordered plus 4 spares.
4. **Weight check before cutting everything.** The first three triangles weigh 55, 56 and 55 g: about 55 g on average. The sheet came out too thin: 3.5 × 60 ÷ 55 ≈ 3.8 mm would be needed. Block 1 is already cut in strips, so its triangles are cut 12 cm wide instead of 11 (weight × 12 ÷ 11 ≈ 60 g); the operator sets the sheeter gap to give 3.8 mm for block 2.
5. **Count check.** At 12 cm wide, a 176 cm strip gives (176 − 6) ÷ 6 = 28 triangles: 56 from block 1. Block 2 at 11 cm and 3.8 mm: 4,100 ÷ (62 × 0.38 × 1.05) ≈ 166 cm, trimmed to 162 cm: (162 − 5.5) ÷ 5.5 = 28 per strip, 56. Total **112**: 8 short. Decision: tell the head baker at once, so the shop knows before opening that 112 croissants will come out; the trims are baked as twists under the house rule, not passed off as croissants. Recorded for the end-of-day report (lesson [11.7](lesson-07.md)).
6. **Yield.** Pieces 112 × 60 g ≈ 6,720 g of 8,200 g of laminated dough: 82 % usable instead of the 90 % planned. Next week: check the sheeter gap on a test strip before rolling the whole block.

## Practice

You roll the block from lesson [11.4](lesson-04.md), cut 16 triangles of about 60 g and roll 16 croissants: 12 for your evaluation batch and 4 for tests in lesson 11.6. If you proof and bake today, go straight on to lesson [11.6](lesson-06.md); if not, see the overnight option there.

> [!WARNING]
> You cut with a sharp knife or a wheel: cut on a board, blade moving away from your other hand, fingers flat on the ruler and above the blade's path. Put the knife down flat and away from the bench edge between cuts. The dough contains wheat and milk.

### You need

- Minimum: rolling pin, ruler (50 cm if possible) or a cardboard triangle template 11 × 30 cm, a long sharp knife or a pizza wheel, scale (1 g), two baking trays with baking paper, cling film, pastry brush, flour for dusting, the [bake log](../../templates/bake-log.md).
- Professional equivalent: sheeter, coupe-croissants roller cutter, 40 × 60 cm trays (perforated for viennoiserie), racks.

### Ingredients

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Home batch | Bakery batch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Laminated dough CR-01, after 3 tours simples and the final rest | 227 | 1,135 g (two halves of about 565 g) | 2,270 g |
| Flour for dusting (brushed off) | — | about 20 g | — |

Expected: 16 triangles of about 60 g (about 960 g), trims about 15 % at home (about 10 % on a bakery sheeter with long strips).

### In Israel

Checked 2026-10-08.

- **Bench and heat.** Cutting and rolling 8 croissants takes about 10 minutes: in a 30 °C kitchen the sheet softens in that time. Roll and cut on the frozen tray or marble, one half at a time, the other half in the fridge; if the sheet sticks or the cut layers smear, chill it 10 minutes.
- **Tools.** A pizza wheel (גלגלת לפיצה, *galgelet le-pitza*) and a long ruler are sold in kitchen-supply shops; a croissant roller cutter can be found in baking-supply shops, but it is not needed for 16 pieces.
- **Trays and paper.** Most Israeli home ovens take a tray of about 40 × 35 cm: 6-8 croissants per tray with 5 cm spaces. If you have one tray, keep the second batch in the fridge while the first proofs and bakes (lesson [11.6](lesson-06.md)).

### Steps

1. **Take one half** of the block (about 565 g) from the fridge; keep the other half cold.
2. **Roll** in both directions to about **32 × 51 cm**, about 3.5 mm thick, lifting and letting it fall back loosely twice. If it shrinks back more than 1-2 cm, chill it 10-15 minutes on a tray.
3. **Trim** to 30 × 49.5 cm. Mark every 11 cm along the bottom edge (0, 11, 22, 33, 44) and every 11 cm along the top starting at 5.5 (5.5, 16.5, 27.5, 38.5, 49.5).
4. **Cut one triangle** and weigh it. Target 60 g. If it is more than 3 g light or heavy, roll thicker or thinner (weight is proportional to thickness) before cutting the rest. Write the weight.
5. **Cut the 8 triangles.** Put the trims aside on the tray.
6. **Shape each:** notch 1-2 cm, fold the inner corners out a little, stretch gently to about 34 cm, roll loosely from the base without pressing, tip underneath. Straight or crescent: choose one and keep it for the batch.
7. **Tray:** 5 cm apart, on baking paper. Cover loosely with film that does not touch them. Weigh every fourth croissant: write the weights.
8. **Repeat** with the second half. Twist the trims into 2-4 small pieces on a separate tray.
9. **Next:** proof and bake (lesson [11.6](lesson-06.md)), or cover and refrigerate overnight.

### Targets

- 16 croissants, each 60 g ± 3 g (all alike).
- Triangles 11 cm × 30 cm before stretching; same number of ridges on every croissant; tip underneath.
- Each half shaped within about 15 minutes of leaving the fridge.

### How you know it worked

The 16 croissants look like one batch: same length, same number of visible ridges, tips all underneath, no flattened or squashed tops. At the cut edges of the triangles you could see fine parallel lines of butter. The weighed pieces are within 3 g of each other. If they are not, your log shows where: a sheet rolled thinner at one end, or triangles cut by eye.

### Self-check

- [ ] I rested the sheet until it no longer shrank before cutting.
- [ ] I weighed the first triangle and corrected the thickness before cutting the rest.
- [ ] I cut with straight, single strokes, from mark to mark.
- [ ] I rolled loosely without pressing, tip underneath.
- [ ] My weighed croissants were within ± 3 g.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix now | Prevent next time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triangles shrink and thicken after cutting | Sheet not relaxed | Chill the cut triangles 10 minutes before rolling | Lift and drop the sheet; rest it before cutting |
| Pieces of very different weights | Sheet uneven; triangles cut by eye | Sort and roll similar weights together | Even rolling; ruler marks or a template; weigh early |
| All pieces too light | Sheet too thin | Cut wider triangles (weight × width ratio) | Weigh the first triangle; weight ∝ thickness |
| Croissants unroll in the oven | Tip on top or at the side | — | Tip underneath, touching the tray |
| Dense centre, few visible turns | Rolled tight and pressed; triangle not stretched | — | Stretch to about 34 cm; roll loosely without pressing |
| Squashed, smeared cut edges | Blunt blade; sawing; warm sheet | Chill, change blade | One firm stroke; sharp blade; cold sheet |
| Croissants bend or twist on the tray | Uneven notch; triangle cut crooked | — | Notch in the middle; triangles symmetrical |

## Review

- Roll the final sheet from cold, gradually, and rest it until it no longer shrinks; then trim the edges straight.
- CR-01: triangles 11 × 30 cm from a sheet of about 3.5 mm, about 60 g; weight is proportional to thickness, so weigh the first triangle and correct.
- Alternate the bases, marks every 11 cm offset by 5.5 cm: (L − 5.5) ÷ 5.5 triangles per strip; trims are not re-laminated into croissants.
- Notch, stretch, roll loosely without pressing, tip underneath, 5 cm apart.
- Weights and regularity are checked in the production test: see [The CAP Boulanger Exam](../../references/cap-exam.md).
