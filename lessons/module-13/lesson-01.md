# 13.1 · Mixers

A mixer does in minutes what your arms do in a quarter of an hour, and it also heats the dough, oxidises it and raises flour dust while it does so. This lesson explains how the three mixers of a French bakery work the dough, how to size a batch to the bowl, and how to run one without putting a hand or a lung at risk. You mix the same dough by hand and with a mixer and measure what the machine changes.

## Why it matters

[Module 3](../module-03/lesson-03.md) chose the mixing method (slow, improved, intensive) and [Module 5](../module-05/lesson-03.md) turned its heat into a friction factor. Both depend on the machine: the same 6 minutes in second speed give a different dough on a spiral, an oblique-axis or a stand mixer, and a half-full bowl does not heat like a full one. The référentiel lists the mixers among the equipment whose role and use you must explain (S3.1, and the batteur for viennoiserie in S3.4); a real EP1 paper asked the role of the batteur-mélangeur (see [The CAP Boulanger Exam](../../references/cap-exam.md)). Mixers also cause two of the bakery's serious occupational risks: hands drawn in by a turning hook, and flour asthma.

Watching someone mix does not make you able to judge a dough in the bowl: this module's practice tells you what to compare, and the Academy cannot grade your photos. Compare your results with the targets and the rubric honestly; this self-assessment does not replace the official practical exam.

## How it works

### Three ways to work a dough

![Three bakery mixers seen from above: the spiral mixer, where bowl and hook both turn beside a breaker bar; the oblique-axis mixer, whose fork on a tilted axis folds the dough while the dough turns the bowl; and the planetary batteur, whose tool spins and travels round a fixed bowl](../../assets/m13-mixer-types.svg)

Every mixer does the same physical job as your hands in lesson [03.1](../module-03/lesson-01.md): it **stretches** the dough (étirage), **folds** it back and traps air (soufflage), so the gluten proteins line up into a network. What differs is how hard and how fast:

| | Pétrin à spirale | Pétrin à axe oblique | Batteur (planetary) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Movement | spiral hook turns on a vertical axis while the bowl turns the other way past a fixed breaker bar | a fork on a tilted axis lifts and folds; the dough drags the bowl round | the tool spins on itself and travels round a fixed bowl |
| Speed | 1st speed (frasage) and 2nd speed | slow, often one speed | several speeds |
| Work per minute | high: fast development | low: long mixing | high with the hook; whisk and paddle for batters and creams |
| Heat and oxidation | the most (improved mix about 4-7 °C, intensive 10 °C or more, lesson [05.3](../module-05/lesson-03.md)) | little | high on a long brioche mix (low TB, very cold liquids, lesson [05.2](../module-05/lesson-02.md)) |
| Typical use | almost every lean bread dough | tradition, levain and soft doughs where flavour and creamy crumb matter | brioche and rich doughs, crème pâtissière, small batches |

The fixed rule from lesson [03.3](../module-03/lesson-03.md) does not change with the machine: **stop on the dough** (smoothness, windowpane, temperature), not on the timer. The mixer changes how many minutes that takes.

### Capacity: neither too full nor too empty

Every bowl has a maker's **maximum** load (in kg of dough or of flour, lower for firm doughs) and, in practice, a **minimum**: too little dough sits under the hook, is not stretched against the bowl and warms unevenly. Overloading strains the motor and leaves an under-mixed core. So an order is split into batches that each fall inside the range, and each batch size has **its own friction factor**: a half-full spiral bowl does not heat like a full one (lesson [05.3](../module-05/lesson-03.md)).

### What else sits around the mixer

The référentiel groups the mixer with the equipment that feeds it: the **flour silo** or sack area, the **balance** (scale, lesson [01.4](../module-01/lesson-04.md)), the **refroidisseur d'eau** (water cooler) and the **doseur d'eau** (water meter) that delivers a set weight of water at the calculated temperature. Their job is to make the base-temperature calculation of [Module 5](../module-05/lesson-02.md) come true batch after batch.

### Safety at the mixer

Two hazards dominate. The **hook draws in** whatever touches it: a hand, a sleeve, a scraper. And **loading raises flour dust**, the first cause of occupational asthma in France according to INRS (lesson [01.3](../module-01/lesson-03.md)).

> [!WARNING]
> A turning hook can draw in and break a hand or arm. Never put a hand, scraper or thermometer into a turning bowl; stop the machine and wait until it has stopped completely before you scrape, test or take a temperature. The lid or grid must be closed; a machine that runs with it open is out of service until repaired. Tie back long hair, no loose sleeves or jewellery.

INRS's prevention measures for the mixer:

- choose a mixer with a **solid lid** (capot plein) rather than an open grid where possible;
- pour the **water first**, then the flour low and gently, ideally through a **long sleeve** reaching the bottom of the bowl; never shake the empty sack;
- start in **first speed** with the lid closed; second speed only once no dry flour is left;
- clean the area with a vacuum suited to flour, never a brush or compressed air (lesson [13.6](lesson-06.md)).

Machines must be designed so that dangerous moving parts cannot be reached in use, their safety devices (lid switch, emergency stop) are checked periodically, and the machine is made safe (stopped and isolated) before cleaning or maintenance (INRS ED 4473). The operator's part: use only a machine you have been trained on, test the lid stop before the first batch, report any fault on a [non-conformity report](../../templates/non-conformity-report.md).

## Worked example

Saturday, Boulangerie Au Pain de la Halle. Order: 160 baguettes PC-02 of 350 g (pâte fermentée sheet from lesson [01.6](../module-01/lesson-06.md): flour 100, water 64, salt 1.8, fresh yeast 1.5, pâte fermentée 15 = 182.3 %). The maker's sheet for the bakery's spiral mixer says: maximum 30 kg of bread dough at 60-65 % hydration. Readings at 3:45: flour 20 °C, fournil 22 °C, pâte fermentée from the cold room 8 °C. The bakery's record for PC-02 "improved, full bowl" gives a friction factor of 26 (4 factors, about 6.5 °C of heating).

1. **Dough needed.** 160 × 350 g = 56,000 g (losses left out for this example). One batch of 56 kg is almost twice the maximum: **two batches of 28 kg.**
2. **Flour per batch.** 28,000 ÷ 1.823 = 15,359 g → 15.4 kg. Then water 64 % = 9,856 g; salt 1.8 % = 277 g; yeast 1.5 % = 231 g; pâte fermentée 15 % = 2,310 g. Check: 15,400 × 1.823 = 28,074 g.
3. **Water temperature** (TPV 24 °C, four factors, lesson [05.3](../module-05/lesson-03.md)): 4 × 24 − 20 − 22 − 8 − 26 = **20 °C**.
4. **Mixing.** Water, then flour low through the sleeve; lid closed; frasage 4 minutes in first speed with yeast and salt; pâte fermentée in the last minute; about 6 minutes in second speed; stop, wait for the hook to stop, windowpane test, probe: 24.3 °C. Recorded.
5. **The incident.** During batch 2 an apprentice opens the grid "just for a second" to push dough down with a scraper. The machine stops, as it must. Nobody restarts it with a hand near the bowl: the head baker explains the rule, closes the grid, restarts in first speed.
6. **Next Saturday the order is 60 baguettes (21 kg of dough, one batch).** The bowl is two-thirds full, not full: use the friction factor recorded for that batch size, or measure it and write it down; do not copy the full-bowl 26.

## Practice

You mix the same lean dough twice, once by hand and once with a stand mixer, measure the friction heat of each, and compare the doughs and the bread. No mixer? Do dough A by hand, then dough B by hand with slap-and-fold kneading (more intense), and compare those two.

> [!WARNING]
> Stand mixer: lowest speed to start, bowl locked, head locked down. Switch off and wait for the hook to stop before scraping the bowl or taking the dough's temperature; never reach in while it turns. Keep the cable away from the sink and the edge of the worktop.

### You need

- Minimum: scale (1 g; 0.1 g for yeast if you have one), probe thermometer, two bowls, scraper, timer, the [temperature log](../../templates/temperature-log.md), baking tray, oven thermometer. Optional: a stand mixer with a dough hook and its manual.
- Professional equivalent: spiral mixer with timer, water cooler and water meter, oblique-axis mixer for slow doughs, batteur for rich doughs.

### Ingredients

PD-01 (the course's direct dough from lesson [01.6](../module-01/lesson-06.md)), one batch per dough:

| Ingredient | Baker's % | Dough A (hand) | Dough B (mixer) | Professional batch (15 kg flour) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bread flour (T55 type) | 100 | 500 g | 500 g | 15,000 g |
| Water | 65 | 325 g | 325 g | 9,750 g |
| Salt | 1.8 | 9 g | 9 g | 270 g |
| Fresh yeast (or instant 0.5 %) | 1.5 | 7.5 g (2.5 g) | 7.5 g (2.5 g) | 225 g |
| Total | 168.3 | 841.5 g | 841.5 g | 25,245 g |

### In Israel

Checked 2026-10-09.

- **The mixer.** Home stand mixers (*mikser omed*, מיקסר עומד) sold in Israel are planetary mixers for 230 V; the dough hook is the *vav lisha* (וו לישה). The manual gives a maximum flour or dough weight for yeast dough and the speed to use with the hook: stay inside both; 500 g of flour is within most full-size machines. A food processor (*me'abed mazon*, מעבד מזון) heats a dough very fast: not used in this lesson.
- **Summer water.** In a 28 °C kitchen with flour at 28 °C, dough A needs water at 3 × 24 − 28 − 28 − 6 = 10 °C and dough B about 3 × 24 − 28 − 28 − 14 = 2 °C: use ice, calculated as in lesson [05.3](../module-05/lesson-03.md) (inside the 325 g), or mix in the air-conditioned room.
- **Flour:** a bread flour as in [Flour in Israel](../../references/flour-in-israel.md).

### Steps

1. **Read and write** the flour, room and tap-water temperatures. Target dough temperature 24 °C.
2. **Calculate the water** for each dough (3 factors): A with a hand friction factor of 6, B with a stand-mixer friction factor of 14 (lesson [05.3](../module-05/lesson-03.md)). Weigh both doughs.
3. **Dough A by hand.** Mix, then knead 10-12 minutes to the windowpane (lesson [03.1](../module-03/lesson-01.md)). Note the minutes, then probe the dough.
4. **Dough B with the mixer.** Water, then flour; lowest speed 3 minutes; then the kneading speed the manual gives for the hook. Every 2 minutes **switch off, wait for the stop**, test the windowpane. Note the minutes to the windowpane and the dough temperature. Feel the motor housing: if it is hot, stop and read the manual's rest time.
5. **Calculate each real friction factor**: 3 × dough temperature − (flour + room + water). Write both in the temperature log.
6. **Ferment and bake both doughs the same way**: pointage about 1 h 30 with one fold, divide each into 6 rolls of 135 g, shape, apprêt, bake at 240 °C with steam (lesson [07.4](../module-07/lesson-04.md)) on the same tray, A on the left, B on the right.
7. **Compare** after 1 hour of cooling: volume, crumb colour (creamy or whiter), cell structure, taste.

### Targets

- Both doughs 24 ±1 °C at the end of mixing.
- Minutes to the windowpane and the friction factor recorded for each dough.
- Twelve rolls of 135 g ±2 g.

### How you know it worked

You have two numbers that are yours: your hand friction factor (typically about 3-9) and your mixer's (typically about 12-15 for a stand mixer on 500 g of flour). Dough B reached the windowpane in fewer minutes and came out warmer for the same water; its crumb may be slightly whiter and more regular. If both doughs reached 24 °C, your calculation worked; if not, the gap tells you how to correct the friction factor next time.

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix now | Prevent next time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dough several degrees warmer than calculated | Friction factor of another product, method or batch size; second speed too long | Shorten pointage (7 % per °C, lesson [04.2](../module-04/lesson-02.md)) | One friction factor per product and batch size; time second speed |
| Small batch barely developed, sticky lump under the hook | Below the bowl's practical minimum | Finish by hand or add the batch to the next one | Respect the minimum load; use the smaller mixer or a batteur |
| Motor labours, smell of hot motor, core under-mixed | Bowl overloaded or dough too firm | Stop; divide the batch | Batches inside the maker's maximum; lower for firm doughs |
| Cloud of flour at the start | Flour poured from height, bag shaken, second speed too early, lid open | Stop, close the lid, let the dust settle | Water first, flour low through a sleeve, first speed |
| Machine runs with the grid raised | Lid switch faulty or bypassed | Stop; take the machine out of service | Test the lid stop before the first batch; report it in writing |

## Review

- Spiral: bowl and hook turn, fast development, most heat; oblique-axis: gentle, cool, long; batteur: tool spins and orbits a fixed bowl, for rich doughs and creams.
- Stop on the dough, not the timer: the machine changes the minutes, not the target.
- Split orders into batches inside the bowl's capacity; each product and batch size has its own friction factor.
- Never a hand or tool in a turning bowl; lid closed; water first, flour low, first speed; vacuum, never blow, the flour.
- Exam-relevant (S3.1, S3.4 equipment): see [the CAP exam reference](../../references/cap-exam.md).
