# 15.1 · Exterior Quality

Before anyone cuts or tastes a bread, its outside has already been judged: by the baker at the oven door, by the sales staff at the shelf and by the customer through the window. This lesson turns that first look into a method you can repeat: a fixed order of checks, measurements instead of adjectives, and a colour scale with a target for each product. You rate the exterior of your own recent bakes.

## Why it matters

The référentiel lists the quality of the product made, "aspect, régularité, goût", as an expected result of the baker's work, and asks you to check the visual aspect of finished products so that any defects are identified (C3.2). In a shop the outside sells the bread: in an INRAE study, consumers were first asked to rate each baguette from its look alone, because appearance is the first information a customer gets and sets what they expect before they taste. How the appearance of finished products counts in the production test is in [The CAP Boulanger Exam](../../references/cap-exam.md).

This module brings together the evaluation you have done product by product in lessons [08.5](../module-08/lesson-05.md) (pain courant), [09.4](../module-09/lesson-04.md) (tradition) and [11.7](../module-11/lesson-07.md) (croissants), and makes it one method for every product. As in every practical module: watching or reading is not doing; the Academy cannot grade your photos, so you compare your result honestly with the [quality rubric](../../templates/quality-rubric.md); and this self-assessment does not replace the official exam.

| pain cintré | *pan san-TRAY* | bread burst along the side, often a sign of under-proofing |

## How it works

### A fixed order, from far to near

Judge the exterior in daylight or under white light, at least 30 minutes after baking for bread (20 minutes for viennoiserie), and always in the same order so you do not forget a criterion:

1. **Count.** Is the number right for the order? Are any pieces missing or set aside?
2. **Regularity of the batch.** Put the pieces side by side: same length, same thickness, same colour? One odd piece is a piece problem; a pattern (one corner darker, the last tray flatter) is a process problem.
3. **Shape.** As ordered (baguette, bâtard, boule, épi), symmetrical, round in section, ends as wanted.
4. **Volume (développement).** Light in the hand for its size; no flat pieces; no tight, small pieces.
5. **Scoring (grigne).** Cuts opened evenly, with an ear on baguettes and tradition; no side tears.
6. **Crust colour.** On the 1-5 scale below, against the product's target.
7. **Crust texture.** Thin and crackling for crusty bread; soft and thin for pain de mie and brioche; even gloss on egg-washed products.
8. **Bottom (semelle).** Baked through, golden; not pale, not black.

### Measure instead of describing

"Nice and long" cannot be compared from one bake to the next; "37, 38, 38 cm" can. Use a ruler, the scale and the colour scale:

| Criterion | What to measure | Example target |
|---|---|---|
| Regularity of length | length of every piece; spread = longest − shortest | home baguettes about 38 cm, within about ± 2 cm (lesson [08.3](../module-08/lesson-03.md)) |
| Shape of a round loaf | width and height; height ÷ width | compare with your best loaf of the same formula: a flat loaf has a clearly lower ratio |
| Volume | weight in the hand against size; optionally, volume by rice displacement ÷ weight | same formula, same weight: the larger volume is the better-developed loaf |
| Crust colour | score on the 1-5 scale; note where it differs | each piece within the product's range |
| Weight | cooled weight against the expected baked weight | lesson [15.4](lesson-04.md) |

**Rice displacement (optional).** Fill a box with dry rice to the brim and level it; empty it into a jug; put the loaf in the box and pour the rice back around it, levelling again; the rice left over in the jug, measured in a measuring jug, is the loaf's volume in mL. Divided by the loaf's weight it gives mL per gram. The number is only useful for comparing your own loaves with each other, not with a table.

### Crust colour

Colour is chemistry you can see. While water evaporates the surface stays near 100 °C; once it has dried it heats further, and above about 105 °C the Maillard reaction between reducing sugars and amino acids starts to brown it; at about 160 °C sugars caramelise. So colour needs three things: a hot enough oven, enough time and **sugars left in the dough**. An over-fermented dough has used up its sugars and stays pale even in a hot oven (lesson [04.7](../module-04/lesson-07.md)).

![The course's 1-5 crust colour scale from pale to dark brown, with the drying, Maillard and caramelisation stages above it, the target range for pain courant, tradition, campagne, pain de mie, viennois, croissant and brioche, and the usual causes of too pale and too dark](../../assets/m15-crust-colour-scale.svg)

The scale is this course's convention for comparing your bakes; it is not an official grading. Not every product aims for the same colour: King Arthur Baking describes sandwich loaves as rich golden brown and brioche as deep mahogany, while crusty loaves go darker.

### The named exterior defects

The référentiel asks you to recognise five bread defects. Here is what each looks like from the outside; their causes are taught where the stage is taught.

| Defect | What you see | Where it is explained |
|---|---|---|
| Pain plat | spread, low, flat cuts | over-fermentation, weak shaping: [04.7](../module-04/lesson-07.md), [08.5](../module-08/lesson-05.md) |
| Pain cloqué | small blisters on the crust | wet pieces from a humid proof, too much steam, long cold retarding: [07.5](../module-07/lesson-05.md) |
| Pain cintré | burst along the side, below the cuts | under-proofing, too little steam, cuts too shallow: [07.5](../module-07/lesson-05.md) |
| Pain ferré | burnt, hard bottom | sole too hot: [07.5](../module-07/lesson-05.md), [08.5](../module-08/lesson-05.md) |
| Croûte terne | dull, matt crust | no steam, skinned dough, over-proofing: [07.4](../module-07/lesson-04.md) |

Viennoiserie adds its own exterior checks: visible, regular layers, even gloss from the egg wash and no butter on the tray (lesson [11.7](../module-11/lesson-07.md)). The general fault tables are in [Troubleshooting](../../references/troubleshooting.md).

## Worked example

Saturday 6:40, Boulangerie Au Pain de la Halle. Before the first load goes to the shop you check the exterior of 24 PC-02 baguettes (350 g pâtons, two decks of 12).

1. **Count:** 24. Order covered.
2. **Regularity:** lengths of every fourth piece: 55, 56, 54, 55, 53, 56 cm. Spread 3 cm: even. Colour: 20 pieces at 4 on the scale; the four pieces at the back right of deck 2 at 3.
3. **Shape and volume:** all round in section; one piece visibly flatter and wider than the others (it was the piece re-shaped at 4:40).
4. **Grigne:** five cuts open with an ear on 22 pieces; two pieces from deck 2 with the last two cuts barely open.
5. **Crust texture:** crackles when pressed; slight shine. **Bottom:** golden on all.
6. **Reading the pattern:** the lighter colour and poorly opened cuts are on the same deck, in the same corner. That is not dough (it is the same dough as deck 1): it points to the oven. The deck log shows deck 2 was loaded 6 minutes after deck 1 with less time to recover after the door was open, and steam was given once instead of twice.
7. **Exterior score** (rubric, exterior lines only: shape, volume, grigne, colour, crust texture, bottom, out of 12): deck 1 typical piece 12/12; deck 2 back-right piece 9/12 (colour 1, grigne 1, volume 1 for the flat one).
8. **Action:** all pieces go to the shop; note in the batch record and tell the head baker: "deck 2 back right paler (3 instead of 4) and two grignes closed; loaded 6 min after deck 1, single steam. Next load: wait for deck 2 to recover, steam twice."

## Practice

You rate the exterior of your own bakes with the fixed order, the measurements and the colour scale. Use your most recent bakes from modules 8-12 if you still have them, or bake one batch of home baguettes (PD-01 or PC-02, lesson [08.4](../module-08/lesson-04.md)) for this lesson; the project of this module will use the method again.

> [!WARNING]
> If you bake for this lesson: dry oven gloves, steam only from a preheated metal tray (never a glass dish), pour the water and stand back. Score with the blade moving away from your fingers.

### You need

- Minimum: your bakes (at least three pieces of one product, ideally two different products), a ruler or tape, a scale (1 g), the [quality rubric](../../templates/quality-rubric.md), your [bake log](../../templates/bake-log.md), good daylight or a white lamp. Optional: dry rice, a box and a measuring jug for volume.
- Professional equivalent: the batch check at the oven door and at the rack before the shop, with the bakery's quality grid and batch record.

### Ingredients

None new: you judge what you baked. If you bake a batch for this lesson, use the PD-01 or PC-02 formula from lesson [08.1](../module-08/lesson-01.md) on 500 g of flour (three baguettes of 270 g).

### In Israel

Checked 2026-10-09.

- **Light.** Judge colour in daylight away from direct sun, or under a white (cool-white) lamp; warm yellow kitchen lights make every crust look one step darker on the scale. Use the same place every time.
- **Humidity.** On a humid coastal day a crisp crust softens within an hour or two (lesson 08.5): judge crust texture between 30 minutes and 1 hour after baking and write the weather in your log.
- **A comparison piece.** Buy one baguette (באגט, *baget*) from a bakery that bakes on site and rate its exterior next to yours with the same scale. You are training your eye, not judging the bakery.

### Steps

1. **Lay out** all pieces side by side on a clean surface, in good light, at least 30 minutes after baking.
2. **Count** and write the number against what you meant to bake.
3. **Measure** the length of every piece (and width and height for round loaves). Write the spread.
4. **Weigh** each piece and write it next to its pâton weight (you will use it in lesson 15.4).
5. **Colour:** give each piece a score on the 1-5 scale, and note where it varies (one end, the bottom, the creases).
6. **Score** the exterior lines of the rubric (shape, volume, grigne or layers, colour, crust texture, bottom), each 0-2.
7. **Look for a pattern:** one odd piece, or all pieces from one tray, one side or one end of the oven? Check your bake log for the evidence (loading order, oven temperature, steam).
8. **Name** any defect from the table (pain plat, cloqué, cintré, ferré, croûte terne) and the lesson that explains it.
9. **Write two lines:** the exterior strength of this bake, and one change for the next.

### Targets

- Every piece counted, measured and weighed; length spread within about ± 2 cm for baguettes of one batch.
- A colour score for every piece, within the product's target range or with the cause written.
- The six exterior rubric lines scored for at least one piece of each product.
- One pattern looked for and either found (with its evidence) or ruled out.

### How you know it worked

You can describe your batch in numbers ("three baguettes, 37-39 cm, colour 4, 4 and 3, the paler one from the front of the tray") and someone reading your notes could tell which piece was weakest and why. Your next-bake change acts on one stage, not on "the bread".

## What goes wrong

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix now | Prevent next time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colour scores change from day to day for similar bread | Judged under different lights | Re-score in daylight or under a white lamp | Same place, same light, scale beside the bread |
| Crust judged "soft" but it was crisp at the oven | Judged too late on a humid day; bread bagged warm | Note the time and the weather; refresh one piece in the oven to check | Judge between 30 min and 1 h; cool on a rack, never in a bag |
| All pieces pale despite the right oven setting | Over-fermented dough (sugars used up) or oven cooler than the dial | Check the fermentation notes and an oven thermometer | Judge fermentation by the dough (04.7); oven thermometer |
| One tray or one corner always darker | Hot spot or sole too hot | Swap tray positions mid-bake | Map your oven; turn trays; second tray under for the bottom |
| "Nice loaves" in the notes, nothing to compare next time | Described, not measured | Measure the pieces you still have | Ruler, scale and colour scale every time |
| A single bad piece blamed on the whole process | Batch not compared side by side | Lay all pieces out together | Look for patterns before changing the formula |

## Review

- Judge the exterior in a fixed order: count, regularity, shape, volume, grigne, colour, crust, bottom; at least 30 minutes after baking, in the same light.
- Measure: lengths and their spread, weights, height-to-width for round loaves, colour on the 1-5 scale against each product's target.
- Colour needs a dry, hot surface (Maillard above about 105 °C) and sugars left in the dough: an over-fermented dough stays pale.
- One odd piece is a piece problem; a pattern across the batch is a process or oven problem: look for it before you change anything.
- Appearance in the production test: [The CAP Boulanger Exam](../../references/cap-exam.md).
